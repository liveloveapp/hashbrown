# Rebuild ForeFlight-style airport overlays from open data

ForeFlight does not derive its on-map airport overlays from FAA Chart Supplement PDFs. Its public account is that it has drawn its own geo-referenced diagrams "from scratch" since 2014, and that the "Embedded Airport Diagrams" on its moving map, which shipped in ForeFlight 8 in 2016, are those same in-house diagrams. Since July 2026 an "Enhanced" set at more than 1,200 airports adds centerlines, hold-short bars and gates, but ForeFlight has not named its data source. The Chart Supplement is the wrong source in any case: Bend's (KBDN) current page is text only, the sketches FAA does publish are being retired, and the supplement's airport-diagram section just reprints the d-TPP diagrams. atc can still get accurately sized overlays without any PDF work. Runways should come from FAA NASR runway-end coordinates, which are surveyed to about ±1 ft and also published as ready-made GeoJSON on FAA's ADDS ArcGIS service. Taxiways, aprons and hold-short lines should come from OpenStreetMap. FAA's AM_* airport-mapping polygons, which are survey-quality, should replace OSM at the few regional airports they cover. Among atc's target airports that is only KSEA. Built once per 28-day FAA cycle, OSM surfaces for the whole 250 nm region come to about 186 KB gzipped (runway geometry from FAA adds only a few kilobytes). Leaflet's built-in GeoJSON and canvas renderers can draw it, so no new npm dependency is needed. The OSM-derived file must be released under ODbL with a notice next to it, and the existing on-map OSM credit already covers the rendered view.

## ForeFlight draws its own diagrams; the Chart Supplement claim does not hold

ForeFlight's overlay has a documented history, and the documents point to in-house drafting. In April 2014 ForeFlight shipped **about 400 "ForeFlight-generated, geo-referenced airport diagrams"** for busy airports that FAA does not diagram ([ForeFlight blog](https://blog.foreflight.com/2014/04/22/new-airport-diagrams-from-foreflight/)). ForeFlight said it had **drawn each one "up from scratch"** and was "working to create one for every airport in the U.S." ([iPad Pilot News](https://ipadpilotnews.com/2014/04/foreflight-adds-400-new-airport-diagrams/)). ForeFlight 8 (late summer 2016) moved these onto a data-driven Aeronautical map layer as **"Embedded Airport Diagrams," described as "the same ForeFlight diagrams"** used elsewhere in the app ([foreflight.com/8](https://foreflight.com/8/)). Rendering depends on zoom: "If you zoom in far enough on an airport, you get the airport diagram. Zoom in further and taxiway labels appear" ([iPad Pilot News](https://ipadpilotnews.com/?p=10191)). Hotspots followed in June 2020 as orange highlights over runway, taxiway and apron surfaces ([ForeFlight](https://foreflight.com/enhancements/hotspots-on-airport-diagrams)). **Enhanced Airport Diagrams arrived in July 2026 (v18.6) at 1,200+ airports**, adding taxiway centerlines, hold-short bars, tie-downs and gate labels ([ForeFlight](https://foreflight.com/enhancements/enhanced-airport-diagrams)).

ForeFlight never says "vector," but the behaviour is clearly vector. Labels appear progressively, hotspots are highlighted surfaces, and centerlines and hold bars are styled one by one. No ForeFlight source names the inputs to its drafting. Imagery plus FAA runway data is the only practical way to trace taxiways at small fields, but that is an inference. The 2026 enhanced feature classes look like an AMDB (airport mapping database). They appeared just after Thoma Bravo closed its $10.55B purchase of Jeppesen and ForeFlight in November 2025 ([Flying](https://flyingmag.com/boeing-spins-off-jeppesen-foreflight-to-private-equity-firm); [AvBrief](https://avbrief.com/jeppesen-foreflight-sale-to-close-next-week-report/)). Jeppesen sells an Airport Moving Maps database covering 1,000+ airports with gates and hotspots ([Jeppesen product sheet](https://ww2.jeppesen.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/02/Product-Sheet_Jeppesen-Airport-Moving-Maps.pdf)). The similar airport counts suggest Jeppesen data sits behind the enhanced set, but this is unconfirmed.

The Chart Supplement claim fails on the facts. The current Northwest supplement page for **KBDN is a single text-only page with no images or drawings**. It gives runway data (RWY 17–35, H5201X75 ASPH) but no geometry ([CS KBDN page](https://aeronav.faa.gov/afd/03sep2026/nw_159_03SEP2026.pdf)). FAA says the sketch blocks are maintained apart from the data-generated text ([FAA Aero Chart Changes](https://www.faa.gov/AIR_TRAFFIC/FLIGHT_INFO/AERONAV/aero_data/Aero_Chart_Changes)). FAA cut the sketches back to runways, numbers and lengths ([FAA 21-01](https://www.faa.gov/air_traffic/flight_info/aeronav/acf/media/Presentations/21-01-IAP-Chart-Modernization-Boll.pdf)), and since October 2023 has been **removing each airport's sketch once its airport diagram gains lighting and NAVAIDs** ([FAA Charting Notice](https://www.faa.gov/air_traffic/flight_info/aeronav/acf/media/Presentations/23-01-Airport-Diagram-Charting-Notice.pdf)).

The strongest reading of the claim is this: the supplement's "Section 5: Airport Diagrams" reprints the to-scale d-TPP diagrams ([NW front matter](https://aeronav.faa.gov/afd/03sep2026/NW_front_03sep2026.pdf)), so someone might loosely call those "Chart Supplement PDFs." But ForeFlight's whole diagram programme exists to cover the airports those PDFs omit, and its own support site notes that some FAA diagrams cannot be geo-referenced because they are not to scale ([ForeFlight support](https://support.foreflight.com/hc/en-us/articles/115010121968-Why-aren-t-some-FAA-airport-diagrams-geo-referenced), seen only as a search snippet). A more accurate statement: ForeFlight digitises its own geo-referenced diagrams and renders them as a zoom-dependent vector layer. FAA diagrams were plausibly a reference, and the enhanced tier probably draws on AMDB-class data.

Competitors fall into two camps. **Garmin SafeTaxi** is a proprietary vector database of more than 2,500 airports, updated every 56 days ([Garmin](https://www8.garmin.com/manuals/webhelp/GUID-7317DC85-4516-4684-BBAF-FD7BE84D5E86/EN-US/GUID-6F890DF7-A1C1-4365-BEFC-DC6283823105.html)). SkyDemon draws ground features into its own chart from national AIP data ([SkyDemon](https://skydemon.aero/start/navigation)). Avare, FltPlan Go and iFly instead **plot ownship on georeferenced FAA PDFs** ([Avare](https://github.com/apps4av/avare); [FltPlan Go](https://imageserver.fltplan.com/FltPlan_Information/FltPlanGOBrochure_3-Page_FINAL.pdf); [iFly](https://www.iflyefb.com/portals/0/documentation/iFly%20EFB%20Pilots%20Guide%2012.pdf)). The open-source WairToNow uses FAA diagrams where they exist and **falls back to OpenStreetMap-backed runway diagrams** elsewhere ([F-Droid](https://f-droid.org/en/packages/com.outerworldapps.wairtonow/)). That is essentially the hybrid recommended below.

## FAA open data covers every runway but only 190 airports' taxiways

FAA's ADDS ArcGIS organisation publishes two kinds of surface data, and both send `Access-Control-Allow-Origin: *` and return GeoJSON ([ADDS services](https://services6.arcgis.com/ssFJjBXIUyZDrSYZ/arcgis/rest/services?f=json)). The first is the **`Runways` layer: 23,368 polygons refreshed on the 28-day cycle**, each a four-corner rectangle built from the NASR runway ends ±width/2 ([Runways layer](https://services6.arcgis.com/ssFJjBXIUyZDrSYZ/arcgis/rest/services/Runways/FeatureServer/0?f=json)). Measured against NASR, the KBDN rectangle comes out at 5,199 × 74.9 ft against a published 5,201 × 75, and KSEA 16L/34R at 11,900 × 149.7 ft against 11,901 × 150. The rectangle ends sit about 1 m from the NASR end coordinates. These rectangles are accurate in size but schematic in shape, with no blast pads, shoulders or fillets. Selecting an airport's runways takes two steps: look up the airport's `GLOBAL_ID` in `US_Airport` by `IDENT`, then filter `Runways` on `AIRPORT_ID`. The similar-looking `RunwayArea` and `RunwayLine` layers are **stale 2016 copies**: KBDN there is still "16" at 5,200 ft ([RunwayArea](https://services6.arcgis.com/ssFJjBXIUyZDrSYZ/arcgis/rest/services/RunwayArea/FeatureServer/0?f=json)).

Underneath sit the NASR CSVs, which carry 8-decimal runway-end and displaced-threshold coordinates, true alignment, length, width and surface ([NASR APT CSV](https://nfdc.faa.gov/webContent/28DaySub/extra/01_Oct_2026_APT_CSV.zip)). FAA's survey standard puts **runway ends and displaced thresholds at ±1 ft horizontal accuracy** ([AC 150/5300-18C](https://www.faa.gov/documentLibrary/media/Advisory_Circular/150-5300-18C.pdf)). NASR also settles a conflict: KBDN is now **17/35**, so OSM's "16/34" tag is out of date.

The second kind is the _*AM_* airport-mapping layers_*: AM_Taxiway, AM_Apron, AM_Runway, AM_Stopway, AM_Hotspot, AM_Building and others. These are DO-272-style polygons "captured utilizing source imagery and verified with data from official sources," and they carry designators such as taxiway `B` or apron "CARGO 2" ([AM_Taxiway layer](https://services6.arcgis.com/ssFJjBXIUyZDrSYZ/arcgis/rest/services/AM_Taxiway/FeatureServer/0?f=json)). FAA started publishing them in August 2025 on a 56-day cadence ([FAA DPN ADDS 25-01](https://www.faa.gov/AIR_TRAFFIC/FLIGHT_INFO/AERONAV/safety_alerts/media/ADDS_25-01_DPN_Open_Data_Release.pdf)), capturing the largest airports first ([ACM 24-02 briefing](https://www.faa.gov/air_traffic/flight_info/aeronav/acf/media/Briefings/Airport_Diagram_Open_Data.pdf)).

Coverage is the catch: **190 airports nationally, and in Oregon, Washington and Idaho only ALW, AWO, PSC, PUW, RNT, SEA and YKM**. KBDN, KRDM, KPDX and KEUG return zero features ([AM_Runway distinct query](https://services6.arcgis.com/ssFJjBXIUyZDrSYZ/arcgis/rest/services/AM_Runway/FeatureServer/0/query?where=1%3D1&outFields=FAA_ID&returnDistinctValues=true&returnGeometry=false&f=json)). At KSEA the set is rich, with 142 taxiway and 22 apron polygons, about 170 KB of GeoJSON in total. The AM runway outlines match NASR length within about 5 ft. **No FAA layer contains hold lines.** The licence says "public use. Reference and Sample purpose only."

The survey-grade AC 150/5300-18 data in FAA's ADIP/Airports GIS includes taxiway holding positions at ±3 ft. But it sits behind registration, and the public API answered "Missing request header 'Authorization'" when tested ([FAA survey update](https://www.faa.gov/sites/faa.gov/files/2022-09/PG_Airport_Geospatial_Survey_Update_2022.pdf); [ADIP](https://adip.faa.gov/agis/public/)). It is off the table for a public web app.

## OpenStreetMap supplies taxiway geometry but labels only a fifth of it

For every airport without AM coverage, OSM is the only open source of taxiway and apron geometry, and its geometry is better than its labelling. An Overpass audit of 15 Pacific Northwest fields on 2026-10-10 found a runway centreline with a `ref` at all of them. **Taxiway and taxilane ways range from 13 at KBDN to 230 at KPDX**, and hold-short nodes exist at the busier fields (100 at KPDX, 63 at KSEA, 12 at KEUG, none at KBDN) ([Overpass API](https://overpass-api.de/api/interpreter)). But **only 309 of 1,645 taxiway and taxilane ways carry a `ref`**: 2 of 13 at Bend, none of 157 at Medford. Taxiway `width` tags are effectively absent. Runway area polygons (`area:aeroway=runway`) exist at about half the fields and are missing at KSEA, KRDM, KGEG, KBOI and KMFR.

Runway centrelines are generally accurate. Lengths fall within about 45 ft of published values, and endpoints sit 1–40 m from OurAirports' coordinates. Three runways are exceptions: the KEUG pair and KCVO 17/35 run about 200 ft long, apparently because pavement beyond the thresholds was drawn into the way. Their `length` tags still give the published figure. US runway refs in OSM often keep leading zeros ("05/23"), and Bend's ref lags the renumbering ([OSM wiki](https://wiki.openstreetmap.org/wiki/Tag:aeroway=runway)).

The practical conclusion is to take runway geometry and designators from FAA and taxiway and apron geometry from OSM. Label only the taxiway segments that carry a `ref`: the labels will be partial, but they will be correct.

**OurAirports** is public domain and regenerated daily ([ourairports-data](https://github.com/davidmegginson/ourairports-data)). It has length, width and surface for almost every regional runway, but **both endpoints for only 288 of 833**, though 82 of 84 at large and medium airports. Its coordinates contain obvious artefacts: all three KSEA runways share one latitude, and KAHC is listed at 10,000 ft while its endpoints are 7,051 ft apart ([OurAirports runways.csv](https://davidmegginson.github.io/ourairports-data/runways.csv)). With NASR available, OurAirports is useful only as a QA cross-check and as an ident-to-name lookup.

The licence question has a clear answer. A bundled GeoJSON of about 10,000 systematically selected OSM features is **a Substantial extract and a Derivative Database, not a Produced Work**, because it is meant to be read as data ([OSMF Produced Work guideline](https://osmfoundation.org/wiki/Licence/Community_Guidelines/Produced_Work_-_Guideline); [Substantial guideline](https://osmfoundation.org/wiki/Licence/Community_Guidelines/Substantial_-_Guideline)). The file must be offered under ODbL, with "© OpenStreetMap contributors" and a link to the licence placed where users would look, such as a README or the file's metadata ([OSMF Attribution Guidelines](https://osmfoundation.org/wiki/Licence/Attribution_Guidelines)). The app's MIT-style code is unaffected. The rendered map is a Produced Work, and atc's existing corner credit already satisfies that requirement; `tiles.ts` already credits OpenStreetMap and links to openstreetmap.org/copyright.

FAA data kept as a separate file, with no join keys pointing into OSM features, forms a Collective Database and stays outside share-alike ([OSMF Collective Database guideline](https://osmfoundation.org/wiki/Licence/Community_Guidelines/Collective_Database_Guideline_Guideline)). The same guideline allows non-OSM data to replace a whole feature type, so FAA runways can replace OSM runways throughout. None of this is legal advice, and a corporate publisher such as LiveLoveApp should confirm it with counsel.

## PDF extraction works at one airport and decays across a cycle

The d-TPP airport diagrams are better raw material than expected, though the pipeline is still a poor trade. First-hand inspection showed the RDM, EUG, PDX, SEA and SFO diagrams are **fully vector, with zero raster images**. At SFO each runway is one black filled polygon, and pavement is drawn as hundreds of fragmented 81% grey fills ([KSFO APD](https://aeronav.faa.gov/d-tpp/2610/00375AD.PDF); [KRDM APD](https://aeronav.faa.gov/d-tpp/2610/00345AD.PDF)). The diagrams are drawn to scale, about **11 m per PDF point (≈1:31,500) at SFO**, a figure confirmed independently from both the graticule and runway length. But they carry **no georeferencing**: none of the GeoPDF or ISO 32000 geospatial keys, no layers and no tagged structure. GDAL therefore has nothing semantic to read ([GDAL PDF driver](https://gdal.org/en/stable/drivers/vector/pdf.html)).

Text handling is inconsistent across diagrams. Taxiway letters are real, extractable text. KRDM's graticule labels extract as text, but SFO's are flattened glyph outlines. Approach plates do embed georeferencing, while diagrams do not, and Garmin has asked FAA for a cleaner per-cycle format ([ACM RD 26-01-411](https://www.faa.gov/air_traffic/flight_info/aeronav/acf/media/RDs/C_26-01-411_IAP_Georeferencing_Data.pdf)).

Precedent stops at georeferenced raster. jlmcgraw/GeoReferencePlates (GPL-3.0 with an added non-commercial clause, last push 2019) and WairToNow (GPL-2.0, last push 2023) fit whole diagrams to map coordinates. **No open project vectorises diagram linework into features** ([GeoReferencePlates](https://github.com/jlmcgraw/GeoReferencePlates); [WairToNow](https://github.com/mrieker/WairToNow)).

The practical route would be to parse `pdftocairo -svg` output, classify fills by colour and size, union the pavement fragments, and fit an affine transform from the runway polygon ends to the NASR coordinates. That is roughly one to two weeks for a handful of airports. The result would have **~10–30 m positional error**, worst on generalised taxiway edges.

Three problems make it fragile. Diagrams change every cycle: SFO's diagram changed between cycles 2610 and 2611, with roughly 490 paths and several taxiway names differing. Lighting symbology is still being added. Polygon union would need a new dependency. The decisive fact for atc is that **KBDN has no airport diagram at all** ([d-TPP metafile](https://aeronav.faa.gov/d-tpp/2610/xml_data/d-tpp_Metafile.xml)), so the home airport gains nothing.

PDF extraction earns a place only as an optional later step. Its one real strength is FAA-current taxiway names: the step would extract the text labels from the diagram, transform them with the NASR fit, and snap each to the nearest OSM taxiway as a naming cross-check.

## A build-time pipeline: FAA runways, OSM pavement, cycle-aligned rebuilds

The recommended pipeline is a single Node script under `examples/atc/tools/`. It uses only built-in `fetch`, `node:zlib` and hand-written geometry (Douglas-Peucker and rectangle offsets are each a few dozen lines), runs at build time, and commits small static GeoJSON files.

Take runway geometry from the ADDS `Runways` layer, filtered to the 250 nm circle. This keeps the CSV unzip step out of the build, though NASR APT_RWY_END stays as an equivalent source if FAA renames the service. The rectangles are true to length and width within a couple of feet, and their designators are current, which fixes KBDN 17/35. Where `AM_Runway` exists, its real outline wins, and `AM_Stopway` adds overruns. Displaced thresholds come from NASR as threshold bars.

Taxiways and aprons come from `AM_Taxiway`/`AM_Apron` at AM airports (KSEA, RNT, PSC and the rest). Everywhere else they come from OSM: `area:aeroway=taxiway` and `aeroway=apron` polygons where mapped, otherwise `aeroway=taxiway|taxilane` centrelines. Once AM coverage is used for an airport, all OSM taxiways and aprons there are dropped. Hold-short markers come only from OSM `aeroway=holding_position`, and hotspots only from `AM_Hotspot`.

Labels follow a fixed order of precedence. Runway-end designators always come from NASR. Taxiway designators come from AM first and OSM `ref` second. Apron names come from AM. OSM `ref`s get normalised: leading zeros are stripped, and any OSM runway feature whose ref disagrees with NASR is discarded.

| Feature                       | Primary               | Fallback                                      | Rule                                     |
| ----------------------------- | --------------------- | --------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------- |
| Runway surface                | AM_Runway outline     | ADDS `Runways` / NASR rectangle               | FAA replaces OSM runways everywhere      |
| Runway designator, thresholds | NASR                  | —                                             | Drop OSM refs that disagree              |
| Taxiway / apron surface       | AM_Taxiway / AM_Apron | OSM polygons, then OSM centrelines            | AM airport ⇒ drop all OSM pavement there |
| Taxiway label                 | AM DESIGNATOR         | OSM `ref` (skip `UNK`, skip TIGER road names) | One label per ref per airport            |
| Hold short                    | OSM holding_position  | —                                             | None in FAA open data                    |
| Hotspot                       | AM_Hotspot            | —                                             | KSEA only today                          |

Write two files. `airports.faa.geojson` holds the FAA features and is public domain. `airports.osm.geojson` holds the OSM features under ODbL, with a `LICENSE-DATA` notice and the generator script kept beside it. Neither file stores join keys into the other.

Both files should use 5-decimal coordinates (about 1.1 m), ~1 m simplification and minimal properties. On measured numbers the OSM surfaces layer is **~1.5 MB raw / ~186 KB gzipped for the full 250 nm circle**. FAA rectangles add only tens of kilobytes, since each runway is under 1 KB. KSEA's AM data adds ~170 KB raw.

A budget of **≤250 KB gzipped**, loaded lazily the first time the map crosses zoom 11, keeps the initial page cost at zero. Hangars and buildings would add roughly another half megabyte raw and should stay out.

Rebuild on the FAA 28-day cycle; the next cycle's files are downloadable before it takes effect. Record the NASR cycle, the AM cycle and the OSM base timestamp in each file's metadata, and fail the build if any runway's rectangle sits more than ~50 m from the matching OSM centreline, as a drift alarm. On the map, extend the existing attribution string with "Airport data: FAA" and add a "Not for navigation" line in the airport card. The OSM credit is already present.

Rendering should follow the physics of Web Mercator at Bend's latitude, where a pixel spans **~110 m at z10, 13.7 m at z13, 3.4 m at z15 and 0.86 m at z17**. A 75-ft (23 m) runway is therefore sub-pixel at z10, ~1.7 px wide at z13 and ~7 px at z15. True-size drawing only becomes visible from about z14, so every layer needs a minimum on-screen width below that. The ladder below mirrors openstreetmap-carto, which starts runways at z11 and labels refs only from z15 ([openstreetmap-carto roads.mss](https://github.com/gravitystorm/openstreetmap-carto/blob/master/style/roads.mss)), so the overlay grows in step with the grey tiles underneath.

| Zoom  | Draw                                                                                                                                                                |
| ----- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| ≤ 10  | Nothing beyond existing airport points (the overlay file is not yet loaded)                                                                                         |
| 11–12 | Runway polygons, filled, with a 2 px minimum stroke                                                                                                                 |
| 13–14 | Add taxiway centrelines at `max(1, widthMetres / metresPerPixel)` px (an assumed default width where none is tagged), aprons as light fills, runway-end designators |
| 15    | Add hold-short bars, hotspots, taxiway `ref` labels                                                                                                                 |
| ≥ 16  | Add apron names and gate/parking refs where present                                                                                                                 |

In Leaflet 1.9 this is one `L.geoJSON` per file in a dedicated pane, sitting above tiles and below aircraft markers. Draw it with an `L.canvas()` renderer, because thousands of SVG paths would slow the existing motion loop. Recompute styles on `zoomend` using `metresPerPixel = 156543.03 · cos(lat) / 2^z`. Place labels as non-interactive `divIcon` markers filtered by zoom.

Because atc's tiles are greyscale and already paint OSM runways in grey, the overlay should use a distinct chart-like palette, such as a dark runway fill with a light edge and a muted taxiway tone. Otherwise the small FAA-to-OSM offsets, up to ~40 m on some runway ends, show as doubled edges rather than reading as one surface.

## Conclusion

ForeFlight never had a secret PDF-to-vector trick. Accurate overlays come from geometry that was georeferenced from the start: surveyed runway ends, imagery-traced pavement, or an AMDB. FAA now publishes the first of these for every runway and the last for 190 airports, and OSM volunteers have traced the middle one. That means a small open-source app can match ForeFlight's 2016-era embedded diagrams at the Pacific Northwest's main fields with a cycle-aligned build script and no new dependencies. The real gap is labels, not geometry. OSM names about a fifth of taxiway segments, and only AM data or PDF text extraction can close that.

The variable to watch is FAA's AM rollout. It began with 8 airports in August 2025, had reached 190 by October 2026, and FAA has said more are planned. Each new regional airport it adds (KPDX and KEUG are the obvious candidates) moves that field from OSM pavement to survey-quality FAA polygons, with no change to the pipeline. Checking the `Pending_AM_*` services each cycle is the cheapest way to see that coming.
